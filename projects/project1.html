Website Critiques

Pitchfork

pitchfork.com

  1. What was your first impression of this site?

    Pitchfork's website had a lot of information spread out eclectically, however, it still stayed concise and was easy to follow. Because of Pitchfork's audience and writing style, this worked in their favor, and was overall very well designed.

  2. How does this site organize the flow of information?

    Their homepage has a header with their logo, along with tabs to other pages. Beneath that is their main content, which is sorted into multiple sections, for parts like their reviews, and their reader's reviews, and their featured content. Each of these is set up differently, but share the same general layout with either lists of cards that scroll right to left, or columns that link out to other reviews or pages.

  3. Does the site structure make sense or does it confuse you?

    I would say that I understand most of what their doing, and they follow all of the important design principles - keeping the important information close to the center, etcetera. One part that could be reformatted, however, is in the 2nd section under reviews. On the left is one of their highlighted reviews for a Mastadon album. They have a cool design feature where while in this section, the review on the left stays centered to the page until it's scrolled away from. However, because the section is so short, it only happens for a few hundred pixels before it stops. I would have loved this to go on for longer.

  4. Does the site graphics/layout/multimedia enhance the site or detract from it?

    I think the layout and graphics enhance the user's experience. It shows all of the albums that they review, which often can have different or contrasting styles. Because of this, the website's background colors and fonts are relatively neutral. This lets the album covers speak for themselves, and gives them due focus.

  5. How long did it take for the home page to load? Was the load time reasonable?

    It took about one second to load, which nowadays is pretty average.

  6. Was the text color and size readable? If a background color or image was used, did it enhance or detract from the site?

    As stated previously, the page color and font were clear and legible. The background color was a neutral grey, where the text was black and serif.

  7. Did the site have a navigation bar or other navigation tool? Was the navigation well done?

    The page had a clear header, with both the Pitchfork logo and tabs that linked out to other pages on the website. Its position was absolute on the page, and so it followed if the user scrolled. It seemed clear and well done.

  8. Did the site have a color scheme or look and feel that was repeated on all the pages?

    Yes, the colors and font style were uniform throughout the whole website.

  9. How does the site respond to smaller window sizes (responsive design)?

    Yes, it responded very well. Because the formatting was mainly scrolling containers of cards and columns, if the window was shrunk horizontally, it would both restructure the columns and change the width of the container of cards.

  10. What does this site offer that was unusual or very well done?

    Again, the amount of information that was condensed into a smaller space was very interesting to see. Websites for news networks like CNN and NBC are structured similarly, where they use several columns to contain the cards that house links to their stories. It's very cool to see a website like Pitchfork aggregate their information similarly.
